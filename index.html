Plant Imaging Rig · reconstruction runs 4 to 6

Three cameras, two lamps

A turntable rig was given extra cameras and a second lamp, and asked to rebuild a 95 mm carved Buddha. Every addition was tested against the finished model rather than assumed. This run starts from a real zero for the first time — the platter is homed on a ruler wrapped round its rim — and it produced the best model the rig has made. The second camera has now been re-lensed, re-aimed and re-fitted across three runs, and still cannot contribute. The reason has changed each time, which is the interesting part.

The constraint everything follows from

A turntable cannot look down

Rotating an object about a vertical axis changes where things are in azimuth and nothing else. The vertical component of every direction — to a camera, to a lamp — is fixed before the platter starts turning, and no number of stops will change it.

Thirty-six views of a turntable are thirty-six samples of one horizontal ring. A hundred and eighty would be a hundred and eighty samples of the same ring.

This one fact explains both of the rig's known limits:

  • Silhouettes from a single ring can never bound a surface that faces straight up or down. That is why adding views stopped helping long ago — 36 views leave the hull 23.6% too large, and 180 views only reach 20.4%.
  • A single lamp on that ring makes photometric stereo rank-deficient. Every light direction shares one elevation, so the normal equations have nothing to say about the third dimension.

Every addition tested here attacks that constraint directly. The second lamp sits at a different height. The extra cameras were mounted above the first.

The bug that nearly ruined it

Five stops, one pose

Before any of the measurements below could be trusted, a capture had to be thrown away. It is worth describing because nothing about it looked wrong.

The frames were valid JPEGs of the right scene at the right brightness. They were simply all from the beginning of the run. Pairwise difference between them was 1.9 to 2.8 — sensor noise.

The camera produces frames continuously, but each stop spends several seconds changing lamps and taking a main-camera still, so only about two frames get consumed every four seconds. A backlog builds in the pipe, inside ffmpeg, and in the driver's own queue. Draining the pipe before each grab empties only the pipe; ffmpeg refills it from its own buffers immediately.

It was resolution-dependent, which is how it hid for a whole earlier run. At 1280×720 the drain mostly kept up and cost a little freshness. At 1920×1080 it failed outright.

The fix is a reader thread that never stops consuming, keeping only the newest frame — a thread that always reads cannot fall behind. The lesson is the cheap check that now runs before anything is carved: crop the subject, difference every pair of views, and compare against the camera's own noise floor. A carve built on frozen frames is quietly meaningless, and nothing else in the pipeline would have complained.

Starting from a real zero

The platter now knows where it is

Until this run every capture began wherever the platter happened to be left. It now begins at a printed mark, found optically, and the difference shows in the result.

A 380 mm ruler is wrapped exactly once around the rim, so every angle has a unique reading at 0.947 degrees per millimetre. The lower camera reads it. Zero is the tape's seam, which is the one vertical edge running the full height of the tape and so is trivial to find.

This matters because the rig has no homing switch, no limit switch and no encoder. An earlier attempt to track the rim by template matching refused to act about as often as it acted, because a finely divided scale is the worst possible tracking target: every tick matches its neighbours equally well, so the answer is a coin toss between periods. Its own code asked for the remedy — a non-repeating mark. The printed numbers are that mark.

  • Homing repeats to 0.285°, measured by independently locating the seam over five trials with a quarter turn between them. This drive's uncompensated backlash is about 1°.
  • This run homed to 0.047° in a single move.
  • The main camera's silhouette height now varies 1.3% across the rotation, against 3.6% and 4.1% on the two previous runs.

It also caught something previously invisible: one commanded move of 3,353 steps delivered 300. Open loop that would have silently ruined a capture, and nothing else in the rig would have noticed.

One honest limitation. Per-stop angle logging is built and works, but the setting that reads the ruler is not stable across a session — an exposure measured as correct became unreadable twenty minutes later because the room lighting changed. Homing at the start of a run is reliable; continuous per-stop tracking wants the rig enclosed first.

Each run fixed the previous run's blocker. Run 4: the backdrop did not reach behind the subject. Run 5: it did, but mask coverage was too low to carve with. Run 6: it has a proper lens, it is sharp, its silhouettes are consistent — and the pose still only fits to IoU 0.693.

So this run also tested whether it could at least contribute texture, which needs no silhouette at all. The test compares the colour MAIN and cam A each report for the same surface point, with exposure normalised out:

Disagreement 0.486, against roughly 0.15 where a texture begins to double. Cam A cannot be projected onto this model accurately enough to paint it either.

What this establishes: the blocker is no longer the mounting, the backdrop, the lens or the focus. It is that a seven-parameter weak-perspective pose fitted from silhouettes is not enough for a wide lens at close range at a steep angle. A calibration target is the one thing that would change the answer, and cam A's data is captured and clean for the moment it exists.

One thing that did get fixed
Lighting

Ambient is back, and it still wins

Every stop is photographed three times: both lamps off, lower lamp only, upper lamp only. On run 4 the unlit frames made the best base colour by a distance. On run 5 they were useless — the room lights were off and they arrived at mean brightness 1.3 out of 255, so the two lamps had to be fused to stand in for them. Here they read 42.6, so the good recipe applies again.

Structure comes from the ambient bake, which has the flattest illumination of the three and nothing clipped. Colour comes from a lamp bake as three per-channel gains (R 0.516, G 0.888, B 1.596) undoing the room's tungsten cast. Only three numbers cross over, so none of the lamp frames' hard shadows come with them.

That the right answer swung from ambient, to a two-lamp fusion, and back to ambient across three runs is not a discovery about photography. It is the room's own lighting behaving as an uncontrolled variable, which is why enclosing the rig sits near the top of the upgrade list.

Photometric stereo

The second lamp, again the thing that paid

The lamps sit at −1.1° and +30.6° elevation. Across 36 stops that is 72 light directions spanning two elevations instead of one, and the smallest singular value of the accumulated normal equations reaches 2.174 — the best of the three runs, after 1.747 and 2.043, against 0.00 with a single lamp, which is structurally unsolvable rather than merely difficult.

Normals solve for 87% of 2,123,332 texels. Median deviation from the smooth surface is 8.3°, with the 90th percentile at 21.0°, after high-passing away the low-frequency bias the raw solve carries.

What would actually move the number

Calibrate cam A. Three runs have now removed every other objection to it. A checkerboard or ChArUco target on the stage, giving real intrinsics including radial distortion, is the only remaining thing between this rig and a second elevation — which simulation puts at 23.6% hull excess down to 10.2%.

Enclose the rig. The correct colour source and the correct ruler exposure have both changed between sessions purely because of room lighting. That is not a variable worth leaving to a light switch.

A caliper across the carving's width. Height is still the only dimension tied to a measurement; width and depth are whatever the hull produced.

The backdrop is 20 mm too short

Cameras and backdrop are bolted to the same frame; only the subject turns. So a camera either has the panel behind the whole cylinder the subject sweeps out, at every rotation, or it never does. One geometric test per camera, measured at the panel’s own plane:

The model predicts the failure that was actually observed, at the end it was observed at. That is the only reason to trust anything else it says.

Cam A looks down at 39.5°, and its sight line past the base of the subject lands 20 mm below the panel’s lower edge. That is where two of its 36 masks came back truncated. For a panel 150 mm behind the axis, the bottom edge must reach +65 mm above the platter for a level camera, −22 mm at 30°, −61 mm at 40° and −195 mm at 60°. The present panel spans −40 to +360 mm: far taller than needed at the top, short at the bottom.

Recommendations

Where to put the lights, with the cameras and the subject where they are

The cameras, the platter and the panel stay where they are. Only the two lamp bars move, and they move to a different place for each of the three things a capture has to deliver. Every recommendation below follows from a measurement already on this page or in the write-up above.

As it is

Both bars stand on the camera side, 20° either side of the axis, one level with the main camera and one beside cam A. That arrangement was chosen for photometric stereo, and it did make the solve work. It is also the arrangement behind three measured problems: green bounce from the lit panel left the albedo with red minus green of 34.4; the lower lamp clipped 0.65% of the colour frames; and the metallic box came back with reflection tunnels, genus 4 before filling.

The glare, worked out

A flat face turned to a camera reflects a lamp at azimuth a to azimuth −a. A bar centred at 20° would miss the lens, but the bar is 300 mm long at 380 mm, so it spans 43°: its near end sits at −1° and its reflection lands in the lens. The same holds for the upper lamp and cam A. Any lamp must be far enough off the axis that its whole length, mirrored, clears the camera: at 380 mm that means a bar centre beyond 45°, or a bar masked down to a short window.

1. For shape

Light the panel and leave the subject dark. Two lamps beside the panel, 250 mm out from the axis and 40 mm in front of the panel plane, aimed across it at its centre, with a flag on each inner side so nothing reaches the subject directly. The subject is then a dark shape against an even green field whatever colour it is, which is what the backlit run proved: the lamps-off, panel-lit capture scored 0.9601, the best to date. Elevation does not matter here, so the lamps sit at the panel’s mid-height. Cam A still needs the panel to reach −61 mm; nothing about lighting changes that.

2. For colour

Soft, equal, and kept off the panel. Both bars at 45° either side of the camera axis, 380 mm out, 25° up, each behind a diffuser, each with a flag that stops the beam short of the panel. The two must match in brightness, because the bake takes a median across views and a left-right imbalance turns into a gradient around the model. The reason for the flags is measured: the best colour this project has produced came from soft room light with the panel barely lit, and the worst from a lamp that lit the panel and the subject together. If the flags are not practical, moving the panel back to 300 mm cuts its brightness to a quarter for the same lamps.

3. For relief

Photometric stereo wants directions that differ as much as possible and sources that behave like points. Put the low lamp at 50° right and 10° up, the high lamp at 50° left and 50° up, both 600 mm from the subject, and mask each bar to a window about 50 mm wide. The present 4° and 36° solved to a smallest singular value of 1.394, so 40° of separation in height is already known to be enough; the azimuth spread and the distance are what the present layout lacks. The relay has two spare channels: a third lamp from the front at 60° up makes the solve well conditioned in every direction, and the ambient frame at each stop should stay, with the room lamp off.

Three lamp positions means moving the bars between passes, or three pairs of bars on the relay’s channels. The second is the one worth building: every position is fixed to the frame, so nothing has to be re-solved between runs. The room’s own lamp sits behind the panel and should be off for every pass; between two runs it alone moved the unlit frames from mean brightness 36.0 to 1.3.
