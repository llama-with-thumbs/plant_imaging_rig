The rig, in numbers

A working model of the turntable rig, built from measurements where they exist and from photographs where they do not. It answers one question with a number instead of an argument: does each camera actually have a backdrop behind the subject. Then it lists what to change, in the order the evidence supports. The test worth building this for

The backdrop is 20 mm too short

Cameras and backdrop are bolted to the same frame; only the subject turns. So a camera either has the panel behind the whole cylinder the subject sweeps out, at every rotation, or it never does. One geometric test per camera, measured at the panel’s own plane:

CameraSide marginAboveBelowVerdict
MAIN+102 mm+161 mm+102 mmCovered, comfortably
Cam A+102 mm+281 mm−20 mmRoom visible below the subject
Cam B+96 mm+59 mm+187 mmCovered

The model predicts the failure that was actually observed, at the end it was observed at. That is the only reason to trust anything else it says.

Cam A looks down at 39.5°, and its sight line past the base of the subject lands 20 mm below the panel’s lower edge. That is where two of its 36 masks came back truncated, and why the one camera that could tighten the hull contributes 0.24%.

For a panel 150 mm behind the axis, the bottom edge must reach +65 mm above the platter for a level camera, +10 mm at 20°, −22 mm at 30°, −61 mm at 40°, and −195 mm at 60°. The present panel spans −40 to +360 mm: far taller than needed at the top, short at the bottom.

The permanent limit

A cone no rotation can fill

Rotating about a vertical axis cannot change the vertical component of a direction. With the highest camera at 39.5°, any surface tilted more than 50.5° from vertical is only ever seen obliquely. Adding stops samples the same ring more finely; adding elevation is the only move that pays:

  • one ring, 36 views — hull 23.6% too large
  • one ring, 180 views — 20.4%
  • plus a ring at 30° — 10.2%
  • plus a ring at 45° — 8.1%

No elevation fixes a recess: a hollow sunk into the top stayed fully filled from 10° to 60°. Silhouettes respond to the outline, and a concavity never changes one.
